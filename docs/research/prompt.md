# Student Talent Marketplace

## Adversarial Market & Problem Validation Research

### Research objective

You are conducting a **pre-build market validation and product research pass** for a proposed Student Talent Marketplace.

The existing product vision is a working concept, not a validated business plan.

Your job is **not to prove the vision is correct**.

Your job is to determine:

> **Are the problems described in the vision real, frequent and painful enough to justify building a marketplace around them — and if so, what is the smallest and strongest product opportunity supported by evidence?**

Be deliberately skeptical.

If evidence contradicts the product vision, report it.

If an assumption is weak, report it.

If an attractive feature has no evidence behind it, say so.

If the research points toward a different product model, recommend that instead.

A negative conclusion is a successful research outcome.

---

# 1. Product Vision Context

The current concept is a professional marketplace for emerging talent, initially centered around university students.

The thesis is:

> Students may have valuable skills but lack visibility, professional history, trusted access to clients and protected ways to transact.

The proposed platform would potentially allow students/emerging talent to:

- create professional identities
- demonstrate skills
- show portfolios and proof
- discover work
- complete real projects
- receive payment
- accumulate reputation
- build professional history
- become easier to trust and hire over time

On the buyer side, the platform potentially serves:

- individuals
- creators
- online businesses
- startups
- SMEs
- agencies
- organizations
- eventually larger/international clients

The vision intentionally leaves several major decisions open:

- service catalogue
- job marketplace
- matching-first
- hybrid marketplace
- verification model
- skill assessment
- reputation/progression
- categories
- payment structure
- dispute model
- monetization
- acquisition strategy

These are **not locked decisions**.

---

# 2. The Central Question

Answer this before recommending any features:

> **Is there a real marketplace gap between students/emerging talent who can perform useful work and people/businesses who need that work?**

Then determine:

1. What exactly is the problem?
2. Who experiences it?
3. How frequently?
4. How severe is it?
5. How do they solve it today?
6. What does the current solution cost them?
7. Why haven't existing platforms solved it?
8. Who is willing to pay for a better solution?
9. What would make them switch?
10. Does a dedicated marketplace actually improve the situation?

---

# 3. DO NOT START WITH “FIVERR FOR STUDENTS”

Do not frame the entire research around:

> "How can we build Fiverr for students?"

That creates confirmation bias.

Instead investigate the broader problem:

> **Emerging talent needs access to credible opportunities and buyers need ways to discover and trust capable people they don't already know.**

Then determine whether a marketplace is actually the best mechanism.

# 36. Validation Experiments

For every promising hypothesis, design the cheapest real-world test.

Examples:

### Problem test

Interview students who recently tried to find paid work.

### Demand test

Ask real businesses to submit actual work requests.

### Supply test

Recruit students and measure how many complete a professional profile.

### Transaction test

Attempt to complete real paid work manually.

### Trust test

Compare informal payment vs protected transaction preference.

### Proof test

Show buyers profiles with different evidence and ask whom they would hire.

### Matching test

Manually match buyers and students before building an algorithm.

### Category test

Run one narrow category instead of launching ten.

For each experiment define:

- Hypothesis
- Test
- Participants
- Metric
- Success threshold
- Failure threshold
- What decision changes afterward

# 38. Evidence Discipline

Every important conclusion must have evidence.

Label conclusions as:

### FACT

Directly supported by reliable evidence.

### OBSERVATION

Directly observed product/user behavior.

### SYNTHESIS

Pattern supported by multiple sources.

### HYPOTHESIS

Plausible but unproven.

### UNKNOWN

Requires validation.

If two sources disagree, show the disagreement.

Do not average conflicting evidence into a fake conclusion.

# 41. Final Verdict

End with a brutally honest assessment.

Use:

### 🟢 Strongly Supported

Ideas supported by substantial evidence.

### 🟡 Promising but Unproven

Ideas with meaningful evidence but important unknowns.

### 🟠 Weak Evidence

Ideas that currently rely too heavily on assumptions.

### 🔴 Do Not Build Yet

Ideas that should not receive serious engineering investment until validated.

Then answer:

> **If we had never written the original product vision, would the evidence independently lead us toward building this marketplace?**

And:

> **What is the strongest version of the idea that the evidence actually supports?**

And finally:

> **What should the team do next — research further, interview users, manually test transactions, prototype, or begin engineering?**

Do not recommend building the full product simply because the concept is coherent.

The purpose of this research is to prevent the team from spending months building a beautifully engineered solution to a weakly validated problem.
